Machinery, industrial equipment and engineering systems. This is likely the clearest Dutch winner. Commission material specifically identifies machinery as a sector facing tariffs up to 44%, with large 2024 EU export volumes and broad future liberalisation. For Dutch capital-goods suppliers, the economic case is not just tariff elimination, but also easier customs procedures, better conformity-assessment dialogue and stronger legal predictability for service and installation teams. India’s scale in manufacturing, infrastructure, agri-processing, water, logistics and energy transition intensifies that effect. The central operational question is not whether the FTA matters; it is whether the exporter can qualify origin, support local installation/service, and avoid being priced out by local-content criteria in public or quasi-public projects.

Logistics, maritime and port services. Dutch strengths in ports, shipping, multimodal logistics and maritime services align unusually well with both the FTA and the bilateral political relationship. India’s FAQ identifies maritime transport services as an EU-interest sector receiving commercially meaningful commitments, and the Commission Q&A highlights maritime services as a key beneficiary of privileged access to the Indian services market. Dutch logistics groups should therefore see India as both a destination market and a corridor market, including supply-chain redesign away from excessive China concentration. The principal legal residuals are domestic licensing rules, port and customs practice, sanctions and export-control screening on routed trade, and state-level execution risk.

Financial services, insurance, project finance and fintech. India’s official FAQ is notably specific here: India has provided 100% FDI commitments in insurance, 74% in banking services, certain market-access opportunities tied to digital payments and fintech cooperation, and access for fifteen EU bank branches over four years. The Commission in turn characterises India’s financial-services commitments as its most ambitious in any trade agreement. For Dutch banks, insurers, payment providers, captive finance arms and infrastructure financiers, the FTA is thus a real market-access signal. But actual deployment will still be shaped by RBI regulation, prudential licensing, data-governance obligations and domestic sectoral regulation. The commercially intelligent move is staged entry: advisory and distribution first, then strategic partnership, then licensed presence where economics justify it.

Legal and accounting services. The treaty environment will expand demand for transactions, customs, tax, compliance, investigations, infrastructure and regulatory work tied to India-connected business. But India expressly states that it preserved policy space in legal services, and recognition of qualifications will depend on future profession-specific engagement. Dutch legal and accounting firms should therefore position for advisory, foreign-law, compliance, structuring and client-support mandates connected with expanding India trade, while not assuming full domestic-practice liberalisation. In the medium term, the most promising growth area is cross-border corporate support: contract re-papering, customs/origin disputes, M&A, JV governance, transfer pricing, ESG due diligence, sanctions and investigations.

Renewables, water and environmental services. India’s scale in energy transition and infrastructure makes this a natural Dutch target, and India’s FAQ identifies environmental services as a commercially meaningful EU-interest sector. That said, project bankability in India is often shaped more by land, permits, offtake, payment security, state power-sector conditions, procurement rules and local-content preferences than by headline treaty text. The FTA improves the environment for equipment export and services provision, but project developers and EPC suppliers will still need serious financing and risk-transfer architecture. For Dutch water, offshore wind, green-hydrogen, grid and circular-economy players, the most robust approach is consortium-based: combine technology, local execution, bankability support and public-risk mitigation.

Pharmaceuticals and medical devices. The Commission identifies pharmaceuticals and medical devices as major beneficiaries. Tariff gains matter, but regulatory and procurement frictions often matter more: registrations, quality control, post-market surveillance, pricing regulation, hospital procurement and local-content preferences. The FTA improves the IP and enforcement environment and reduces some border costs, but medtech and pharma firms should still operate India as a regulated market requiring product-registration strategy, channel control, hospital tender management and robust anti-diversion measures.

Automotive and mobility. The agreement’s most visible political concession is the staged reduction of Indian auto tariffs from 110% to 10% within a quota of 250,000 vehicles a year. That makes the agreement commercially relevant for premium vehicles and certain components, but the sector remains highly political and structurally linked to India’s industrial policy. For Dutch companies, the better opportunities may be in automotive electronics, specialised components, charging infrastructure, logistics, testing, tooling and high-value niche imports, rather than in broad-volume finished-vehicle trade.

ICT, software and digital services. India is already the Netherlands’ largest Asian source of services imports, particularly computer services and business services, according to CBS. That fact, combined with the FTA’s digital-trade chapter and source-code protection, creates a strong basis for deeper Dutch–Indian digital integration. Yet domestic legal compliance remains non-trivial because India’s DPDP Act preserves governmental power to restrict certain cross-border transfers and imposes additional obligations on Significant Data Fiduciaries. The correct strategic response is not to avoid India, but to build layered data architecture: classify data, identify regulated sectors, segment processing, localise only where required, and contract carefully with Indian processors and clients.

Execution toolkit for Dutch firms

The right execution model is not a single “India strategy” but a decision tree linking product or service, origin profile, regulatory burden, financing structure and dispute path. The decision process below refines the earlier draft’s decision model.

flowchart TD
    A[Identify target product or service] --> B{Tariff-led value case?}
    B -->|Yes| C[Map HS code, tariff staging and origin rule]
    B -->|No| D[Map services schedule, licensing and local presence needs]
    C --> E{Can origin be qualified reliably?}
    E -->|Yes| F[Model tariff savings and re-price route to market]
    E -->|No| G[Consider EU sourcing shift, local assembly or JV]
    D --> H{Domestic regulation tolerable without local entity?}
    H -->|Yes| I[Export or cross-border service model]
    H -->|No| J[Subsidiary, branch, distributor, JV or acquisition]
    F --> K[Assess customs, SPS/TBT, data, sanctions and export-control compliance]
    G --> K
    I --> K
    J --> K
    K --> L{Public tenders relevant?}
    L -->|Yes| M[Run local-content and tender governance workstream]
    L -->|No| N[Commercial contracting and channel controls]
    M --> O[Structure finance, insurance and risk transfer]
    N --> O
    O --> P[Board approval with scenario triggers and escalation plan]

This decision model reflects the logical sequence implied by the FTA’s architecture and India’s domestic procurement and data-governance environment: one first determines whether the business case is tariff-led or services-led, then whether origin or licensing can actually be operationalised, and only then whether exporting, partnering or investing is the optimal structure.

Financing and export credit

Financing should be treated as a primary structuring variable, not an afterthought. The Dutch state’s broader export and strategic-goods architecture is designed to support high-value, internationally oriented firms under a rules-based control environment, while private-market tools such as trade-credit insurance, specialist trade-finance support, debt collection and surety can materially improve cash-flow resilience and contractability. Dutch export-control policy formally recognises the importance of trade to high-value Dutch industry while underscoring that strategic and dual-use exports require licensing and close supervision through customs and the Ministry of Foreign Affairs; Atradius as a group offers credit insurance, specialist trade-finance support, debt collections and surety products that are directly relevant to India receivables and project execution.

For large India projects—especially infrastructure, industrial equipment, renewable-energy systems, maritime packages and long-tenor supply contracts—the key bankability test is whether the FTA materially reduces execution risk or simply improves economics at the margin. A lender or credit committee will usually ask seven questions: whether tariffs materially improve DSCR or project IRR; whether origin qualification is realistically maintainable; whether the local-content structure is compliant; whether payment security is robust; whether sanctions and export controls are manageable; whether dispute clauses are enforceable; and whether policy-change risk is insurable or contractually allocated. The FTA helps on several of those dimensions, especially tariff margin, regulatory transparency and services access, but it does not replace hard risk-transfer tools.

The practical financing stack for Dutch companies entering India commonly includes some mix of: private trade-credit insurance for open-account sales; standby letters of credit or documentary structures for new counterparties; advance-payment bonds or performance bonds for EPC/public-sector work; receivables purchase or bill discounting; political-risk insurance for long-tenor or quasi-sovereign exposure; and, where available and suitable, Dutch public export-credit support through the state’s export-credit architecture involving Atradius Dutch State Business. Even where a company does not use public cover, the discipline required to prepare an export-credit dossier—robust KYC, sanctions screening, environmental and social review, supply-chain mapping, end-use declaration and dispute structure—improves internal governance and helps commercial bankability.
