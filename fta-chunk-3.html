Warning on no direct private rights

Critical legal warning. India’s official FAQ states that the FTA “does not confer direct rights or impose obligations on private persons, nor does it allow the trade deal to be directly invoked in domestic courts. Rights and obligations under the trade deal exist only between the Parties.” For Dutch companies, that means the FTA is not a substitute for customs appeals, domestic administrative litigation, arbitration clauses, local-law remedies or diplomatic/state-to-state escalation.

The practical legal consequences are significant. If a Dutch exporter is denied preference because Indian customs contests origin, the immediate route is not a direct treaty action in a Dutch or Indian court. The company must instead use the applicable customs-origin procedure, documentary correction, administrative review, and—if the problem is systemic rather than transaction-specific—seek escalation through industry bodies, the Dutch authorities, the European Commission or, at the interstate level, the FTA’s committee and dispute-settlement structures. Similarly, if a regulatory measure frustrates expected market access, the company’s operational remedies remain contractual adaptation clauses, local-law challenges, licensing strategy, restructuring, and coordinated government engagement.

This point also explains why dispute-resolution clauses in private contracts remain indispensable even after the FTA. The treaty can improve the legal environment, reduce tariffs and strengthen transparency, but it does not remove the need for a properly drafted law-and-forum clause, regulatory-change allocation, customs cooperation duty, data-localisation fallback and payment-security framework. In-house counsel should therefore resist the common but erroneous assumption that “the FTA will protect us” in a directly enforceable sense. It will not—at least not in the way EU companies sometimes colloquially imagine.

Market access, tariffs, regulation and dispute settlement

The tariff bargain is commercially large by EU–India standards. Official Commission material records that the EU will eliminate tariffs on over 90% of tariff lines and 91% in value terms, while India will eliminate tariffs on 86% of tariff lines and 93% in value terms; with partial liberalisation on additional lines, the overall coverage rises to 99.3% for the EU and 96.6% for India. The economic logic is straightforward: India has historically combined a large market with relatively high border protection in many EU export sectors, so even partial liberalisation has disproportionately high value for exporters that previously faced borderline-prohibitive tariffs.

Trade data trends relevant to Dutch firms

CBS reports that Dutch imports of goods and services from India rose to €17.4 billion in 2024, with goods imports of €7.23 billion and services imports of €6.58 billion, compared with €2.85 billion and €3.98 billion respectively in 2020. The trend is significant because it shows that India is already embedded in Dutch trade and services supply chains before any FTA preferences come into force; the agreement therefore modifies a relationship that is already commercially meaningful, rather than creating one from scratch.
