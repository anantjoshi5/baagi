Executive summary

The EU–India Free Trade Agreement has moved from a long-running negotiation to a legally advanced but not yet fully binding instrument. EU and Indian leaders resumed negotiations in May 2021, accelerated them in February 2025, and concluded negotiations on 27 January 2026. The European Commission has published the negotiated texts subject to legal revision; India’s Ministry of Commerce has likewise published a detailed official FAQ, and in July 2026 public statements indicated that legal scrubbing was nearing completion. The agreement will only become binding after completion of internal procedures on both sides, so Dutch companies should treat the present text as highly probative but not yet final in article numbering or legal drafting detail.

For Dutch business, the commercial significance is considerable. The Commission states that bilateral EU–India goods trade reached €120 billion in 2024 and services trade €59.8 billion, while the agreement is expected to reduce or eliminate tariffs on over 90% of EU goods exports and save European exporters up to €4 billion annually in duties. The Dutch angle is especially material because the Netherlands is already the EU’s second-largest importer of Indian goods and services and the largest importer of Indian goods, while India has become an increasingly important source of Dutch services imports in computer, consulting and technical business services. Even where Dutch exports to India do not dominate current trade statistics, Dutch firms are structurally exposed through re-export, logistics, shipping, technology licensing, project finance, business services and intra-group value chains.

The agreement is most attractive for Dutch companies in machinery, chemicals, life sciences, medical technology, maritime services, logistics, environmental services, semiconductors and advanced manufacturing. Official Commission material identifies strong benefits for agri-food, chemicals, pharmaceuticals, machinery, medical devices, avionics and automotive industries. India’s official FAQ confirms commercially meaningful market-access and national-treatment commitments across 102 services sectors and subsectors, and highlights maritime transport, financial services, telecommunications and environmental services among sectors of EU interest. For Dutch firms, this combination matters because Netherlands–India cooperation is already being institutionalised through bilateral customs assistance, a Joint Trade and Investment Committee, semiconductor cooperation and critical-minerals cooperation.

Tariff savings will be real, but the legal and commercial value of the FTA lies just as much in non-tariff predictability. The negotiated text and official summaries indicate disciplines on rules of origin, self-certification of origin, customs cooperation, advance rulings, simplified procedures, expedited release, SPS timelines, TBT transparency, conformity-assessment dialogue, digital trade rules, source-code protection, IP enforcement, competition-law maintenance, subsidy transparency, SME contact points, good regulatory practices and state-to-state dispute settlement. For Dutch companies that currently lose time and margin in India because of certification delays, local-testing frictions, changing Quality Control Orders, customs valuation disputes, entity-structuring constraints and licensing uncertainty, these process commitments may be nearly as valuable as the headline tariff cuts.

The right legal posture is cautiously favourable rather than euphoric. India remains a large, strategically attractive and increasingly reform-oriented market, and the FTA materially improves the treatable risk profile of Dutch market entry. But the agreement does not abolish Indian regulatory autonomy, does not erase domestic licensing or localisation rules, does not neutralise export controls or sanctions risk, does not guarantee public procurement access comparable to GPA-style disciplines, and does not create directly enforceable treaty rights for private parties in domestic courts. India’s own official text expressly states that the FTA creates rights and obligations only between the Parties and cannot be directly invoked by private persons or companies in domestic courts. That is a critical legal point for Dutch boards, GCs and deal teams: a treaty preference that is misapplied in practice usually has to be pursued through customs procedures, administrative remedies, contractual protections, domestic litigation or arbitration, and—at system level—through state-to-state channels rather than direct treaty litigation by the company itself.

Three scenarios therefore matter for decision-making. In a comprehensive implementation scenario, the legal scrub is completed in 2026, signature follows, and the agreement enters into force or is provisionally applied on a commercially meaningful timeline; Dutch firms should re-paper contracts, qualify origin, map tariff savings and prepare India-specific go-to-market investments. In a limited or delayed implementation scenario, signature occurs but ratification is slower, some annexes or schedules shift in legal revision, and preference utilisation is staggered; firms should still prepare, but capital deployment should be phased and conditional. In a no-deal or prolonged standstill scenario, current MFN tariffs, localisation frictions and sectoral restrictions remain; in that case, India still merits attention for Dutch companies, but market entry economics will rely more heavily on local assembly, joint ventures, licensing, services delivery and financing structures rather than border liberalisation. The report below analyses all three scenarios expressly because the final implementation date has not yet been fixed in binding law.

A key quantitative conclusion is that the Commission’s “up to €4 billion annually” estimate and a higher “ceiling” estimate are not contradictory; they measure different things. Using the Commission’s own table of selected 2024 EU exports to India and the current tariff caps for those sectors, the theoretical maximum gross duty exposure for that sample is about €12.56 billion. That is not an expected annual saving. It is an upper-bound illustration assuming that every listed sector paid the maximum cited tariff on the full trade value, without tariff-rate quotas, exclusions, staging, utilisation frictions, sectoral mix effects, valuation adjustments or substitution. The Commission’s €4 billion figure is a far more realistic aggregate estimate after accounting for product mix, phase-ins, carve-outs and actual payable duties. For Dutch boards, the practical implication is that internal business cases should use conservative, utilisation-adjusted estimates rather than political headline numbers.

For Dutch firms, the most commercially prudent immediate actions are fivefold. First, classify products and services accurately and map them to the negotiated schedules and likely rules of origin. Secondly, review channel strategy: direct export, local subsidiary, branch, liaison office, distributor, JV, toll manufacturing or acquisition. Thirdly, re-paper commercial contracts so that tariff preferences, customs data, regulatory-change risk, localisation, certification, procurement status and dispute resolution are contractually allocated. Fourthly, strengthen compliance infrastructure covering customs, origin, SPS/TBT, IT-security, data governance, competition, export controls, sanctions and anti-corruption. Fifthly, treat financing and risk transfer—notably trade-credit insurance, political-risk cover, project-finance structuring, surety and public export-credit support—as an integral part of the India strategy rather than a back-end treasury matter. Those actions are justified whether the agreement is fully implemented quickly or only after a delay.

Assumptions, methodology and scenarios

Assumptions used in this report. Because the final signed treaty text has not yet gone through the full legal-revision and entry-into-force process, this report assumes three legally plausible operating scenarios: Scenario A: comprehensive FTA implementation broadly in line with the published post-conclusion texts; Scenario B: limited or delayed implementation, where signature and entry into force are slower, some annexes are refined in the legal scrub, and operational benefits phase in unevenly; and Scenario C: no deal or extended non-ratification, where current WTO/MFN conditions essentially persist. The report also assumes that product-specific rules of origin and annex numbering may still be adjusted in legal revision, because the Commission expressly states that the published texts are for information purposes and may undergo further modification until signature.

Methodology for quantitative estimates. All quantitative estimates are intentionally conservative and are designed for board-level decision support, not for customs declarations or valuation opinions. The report prioritises official data from the European Commission, India’s Ministry of Commerce and Industry, CBS/Eurostat and other public authorities. Where official sources provide ranges or sector-level tariff caps rather than product-line applied rates, the report distinguishes between: headline political estimates, ceiling estimates, and firm-specific utilisation-adjusted estimates. The Commission’s own “up to €4 billion per year” saving is treated as the official policy estimate; the separate €12.56 billion figure is treated as an illustrative upper bound derived from the Commission’s selected sector table, not as a forecast.

The core formulas used are as follows:

  • Gross theoretical duty exposure
    Duty exposure ceiling = Σ (Trade value in sector i × tariff cap in sector i)
  • Expected gross tariff saving at firm level
    Expected saving = CIF customs value × preferential margin × preference-utilisation rate
  • Post-FTA net price effect for importer
    Net landed-cost effect = customs duty saving – preference administration cost ± certification/structuring cost ± logistics offset
  • If import GST/VAT is creditable
    P&L benefit is usually closer to customs-duty saving than to total cash-at-border saving, because indirect tax may be recoverable under domestic law, whereas customs duty usually is not.
