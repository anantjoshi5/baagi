These formulas are standard trade-policy analytics, but the report deliberately avoids false precision where product-level tariff lines, quotas or exemptions remain unsettled or confidential to the company.

Worked example of the €12.56 billion ceiling. The Commission’s Q&A identifies selected EU exports to India in 2024, including machinery and electrical equipment (€16.3 billion, current tariffs up to 44%), aircraft and spacecraft (€6.4 billion, up to 11%), optical/medical/surgical equipment (€3.4 billion, up to 27.5%), plastics (€2.2 billion, up to 16.5%), pearls/precious stones/metals (€2.1 billion, up to 22.5%), chemicals (€3.2 billion, up to 22%), motor vehicles (€1.6 billion, 110%), iron and steel (€1.5 billion, up to 22%) and pharmaceuticals (€1.1 billion, 11%). Multiplying each sector’s trade value by the relevant tariff cap and summing the results produces an upper-bound duty exposure of roughly €12.56 billion. That number is intentionally overstated because it assumes that all trade in each sector pays the maximum cited tariff and ignores phase-ins, product mix, exclusions, quotas, preferences and existing exemptions.

Why the €4 billion Commission estimate is the right benchmark for macro analysis. The Commission’s “up to €4 billion per year” estimate is load-bearing because it reflects the negotiated legal outcome rather than a mechanical multiplication of sector values by tariff caps. It implicitly incorporates the fact that many tariff lines are already lower than the sectoral maximum, some products liberalise only partially or over time, some benefits depend on tariff-rate quotas, and preference utilisation is never 100% in the first years of a new agreement. For internal Dutch corporate modelling, the Commission number is therefore the appropriate macro baseline, while the higher ceiling estimate is useful only to show the outer envelope of tariff exposure in sectors where India’s tariffs have historically been very high.

Preference-utilisation modelling. Preference utilisation will depend on origin qualification, documentary quality, customs confidence, commercial incentive and channel structure. The Commission summary indicates that proofs of origin will be based on self-certification and statement-on-origin documentation uploaded to a portal for customs verification, with administrative cooperation between EU and Indian customs before preferences may be refused. Self-certification reduces direct administrative friction, but utilisation will still lag if Dutch exporters rely heavily on non-originating Asian inputs, if product engineers cannot demonstrate substantial transformation, or if commercial teams fail to align Incoterms and customs-responsibility clauses. For first-year modelling, prudent firms usually apply a utilisation assumption below theoretical eligibility.

Short worked example for a representative Dutch machinery exporter. Assume a Dutch machinery company ships one production line to India on an annual contract. FOB Rotterdam price: €5,000,000. Freight and insurance: €250,000, making CIF India €5,250,000. Assume, as a conservative proxy, that the relevant Indian duty under current conditions is 16%—consistent with the Commission’s statement that Indian duties on industrial products are on average above 16%, even though some machinery lines are much higher. Assume further that the product meets an illustrative product-specific origin requirement because EU-origin value content is 68% and non-originating materials do not exceed the assumed limit. On that basis:
Scenario A, comprehensive FTA: customs duty falls to 0%; gross customs-duty saving = €840,000.
Scenario B, limited/delayed implementation: assume interim tariff cut to 8%; gross customs-duty saving = €420,000.
Scenario C, no deal: no tariff saving.
If the importer can recover Indian import GST as input tax credit, the enduring P&L benefit is close to the customs-duty saving rather than the full cash-at-border movement. If origin-compliance, engineering segregation, supplier declarations and customs administration cost €60,000 annually, net savings remain material in Scenario A and still meaningful in Scenario B. The legal conclusion is that even moderate utilisation can support re-pricing, distributor margin restructuring or local-service investment.

Limitations and caution on interpretation. First, official tariff descriptions in the public summaries are often expressed as “up to” rates or staging ranges, not line-by-line applied duties. Secondly, the legal-revision process may alter article numbering or drafting nuances. Thirdly, import GST, customs valuation, para-tariffs, anti-dumping duty, safeguard duty, testing fees, warehouse costs and local service requirements can materially affect real landed cost. Fourthly, utilisation may differ sharply by business model: a Dutch software or maritime-services firm will care more about regulatory certainty and market access than about border duties, whereas a machinery or food exporter will care deeply about origin and customs processes. These limitations are not defects; they are the reason prudent firms should combine treaty analysis with product-line customs work and commercial structuring.

Negotiation status, institutional pathway and legal architecture

The present status is unusually advanced for commercial planning but still short of legal finality. The European Commission’s India page records that leaders agreed in May 2021 to resume negotiations for a “balanced, ambitious, comprehensive and mutually beneficial” FTA while also launching separate negotiations on an investment-protection agreement and a GI agreement. After acceleration in February 2025, negotiations on the FTA were concluded on 27 January 2026. The Commission then published the text of the agreement, but expressly stated that it is published for information purposes only, remains subject to legal revision, and becomes binding only after completion of each Party’s internal legal procedures. India’s Ministry of Commerce published a detailed FAQ on 29 January 2026, which is likewise expressly informational and may still be modified after legal scrub.

That institutional posture has two practical consequences for Dutch companies. First, the negotiated commercial bargain is sufficiently clear to justify strategic preparation, internal budgeting, channel redesign and counterpart re-papering. Secondly, no board should treat published chapter labels, annex references or timetable assumptions as if they were already the finally signed and ratified law. In July 2026 public reporting indicated that legal review was nearing completion and that signature was targeted by the end of 2026, but until the final instruments are signed and internal procedures close, timing risk remains real.

The legal architecture is broad and commercially significant. The Commission’s published chapter list covers goods market access, rules of origin, customs and trade facilitation, SPS, TBT, trade remedies, services, digital trade, intellectual property, anticompetitive conduct, SMEs, transparency, good regulatory practices, sustainable food systems, trade and sustainable development, dispute settlement, institutional provisions, final provisions and general exceptions. This breadth is the reason the FTA should be understood not simply as a customs instrument but as a framework for market-entry conditions, compliance predictability and inter-agency cooperation. The absence of a dedicated public-procurement chapter in the published chapter list is equally important, because it indicates that procurement access will continue to depend heavily on domestic Indian procurement rules, local-content policies, sectoral exceptions and tender design rather than on deep treaty-style procurement disciplines.
