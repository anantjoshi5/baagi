Competition, state aid and subsidies are addressed in a measured rather than transformative way. The Commission says the competition chapter ensures each Party has competition law applying to private and public enterprises and enforced by an independent authority, with cooperation between authorities and a possibility for the EU to seek information on subsidies raising concerns. India’s FAQ then clarifies that the binding commitments on subsidies relate mainly to transparency and consultations; agriculture and fisheries are excluded; subsidies below a budgetary outlay of 18 million SDR over three consecutive years are out of scope; and the subsidies chapter is excluded from bilateral dispute settlement. The legal meaning is clear: the agreement does not export a full EU-style state-aid control regime into India. For Dutch firms, the practical value lies in transparency, consultation leverage and evidence-building—not in expecting the treaty to police Indian industrial policy in the manner of EU internal-market law.

Transparency and good regulatory practices are more important than they appear. The agreement requires publication of measures of general application, enquiry mechanisms, objective and impartial administration, review and appeal procedures, wide non-discriminatory consultation on major regulatory initiatives, and use of regulatory impact assessments in accordance with domestic procedures. For firms entering or scaling in India, the value of these chapters lies not in courtroom drama but in risk management: better warning before major rule changes, stronger grounds for submission during consultation, and a more robust administrative record if later escalation becomes necessary.

Dispute settlement is state-to-state, but the agreement also creates a Rapid Reaction Mechanism. India’s FAQ states that disputes on interpretation or application may be addressed through consultations in the Joint Committee or specialised committees and, where needed, through the treaty’s dispute-settlement mechanism, without prejudice to the Rapid Reaction Mechanism. The RRM is designed for concerns arising from measures of general application, future or planned regulatory initiatives, and major implementation issues capable of materially impeding bilateral trade, and operates through a three-tier structure: specialised committee, senior officials in the Joint Committee, and ministerial-level review, all on defined timelines. This is commercially useful for Dutch firms because many India issues emerge first as draft regulations or implementation frictions rather than as clean justiciable disputes.

The agreement also contains a formal review clause. India states that the Joint Committee must conduct a general review within five years of entry into force and every five years thereafter, assessing progress in liberalisation and whether mutually beneficial outcomes are flowing from the FTA. Services MFN obligations are also subject to a five-year review mechanism in relation to specified sectors and parameters. For Dutch firms, that means the FTA should be viewed as a dynamic platform whose commercial value can improve—or disappoint—during implementation cycles. Early participation in industry consultation and implementation review will therefore matter.

Agri-food. The agreement offers real but selective upside for Dutch agri-food exporters. Official EU materials identify elimination or reduction of tariffs on olive oil, juices, processed foods, some meats and premium alcoholic beverages, while preserving sensitivities in many politically charged agricultural products. Dutch exporters of processed food, ingredients, premium beverages, horticultural genetics and specialty products should read this as a meaningful but curated opening, not a broad liberalisation of Indian agriculture. SPS timelines and establishment-listing procedures may become more predictable, but food-admissibility work, veterinary or phytosanitary certificates, packaging adaptation and importer capability remain decisive. The Sustainable Food Systems chapter is strategically positive because it creates cooperation without forcing regulatory harmonisation.

Chemicals and specialty materials. Tariff reductions in chemicals and plastics are directly relevant to Dutch exporters, and the Commission identifies chemicals among the principal beneficiaries. Yet chemicals are also exposed to non-tariff frictions, including Indian standards regulation, labelling, testing and sometimes trade-remedy risk. The right operating model for Dutch chemicals companies is therefore dual-track: harvest border-duty improvement where available, but simultaneously build India-specific compliance capacity for product registrations, conformity assessment and distributor/end-use controls. Firms selling controlled precursors or dual-use adjacent items must also overlay Dutch and EU export-control obligations.

High-tech, semiconductors and advanced manufacturing. The bilateral India–Netherlands relationship is already deepening in semiconductors, critical minerals and emerging technologies. In May 2026, the two governments welcomed a partnership on semiconductors and related emerging technology, linkage between the Dutch Semicon Competence Centre and the Indian Semiconductor Mission, and an MoU on critical minerals aimed at resilient and sustainable supply chains. The FTA adds a wider framework of tariff relief, digital-trade protection and predictable services rules. For Dutch semiconductor equipment, design, materials and enabling-technology firms, India becomes more credible as a market, talent base and selective value-chain location. But this sector remains heavily conditioned by export controls, knowledge-security considerations, sensitive-technology governance and carefully segmented IP architectures.
