A Dutch board should treat India IP protection as a layered operating system rather than as a single registration exercise. The FTA’s IP chapter is strong in principle: the Commission says it covers copyright, trademarks, designs, protection of trade secrets and undisclosed information, plant varieties, plus enforcement measures, procedures and remedies. But the practical question for Dutch companies is not whether the treaty says the right things; it is whether the business has engineered its India operations so that registration, confidentiality, manufacturing, data access and enforcement evidence are all mutually reinforcing.

Layer one: registration. Register the trade mark, key logos, house marks, essential product marks and any registrable designs before commercial launch, not after distributor appointment. Where patentable subject matter exists, align India filings with EU/PCT strategy and local working requirements. For digital products, register brands, domain strategy and major user-facing assets; for industrial products, map which design or patent filings actually matter to market entry and tender visibility. Registration is still foundational because it anchors border actions, enforcement letters and due-diligence credibility.

Layer two: trade-secret architecture. Most Dutch competitive advantage in India-facing business will sit not in patents but in know-how: formulations, source code, parameter settings, pricing logic, customer data, manufacturing tolerances, service protocols, implementation playbooks and non-public specifications. Those assets must be segmented by access level, not merely marked confidential. At a minimum, contracts should include clear definitions of confidential information, use restrictions, no-reverse-engineering language where lawful, prompt return/deletion obligations, onward-transfer limits, incident notification and audit rights. Technically, companies should separate demonstration environments from production environments, redact manufacturing tolerances from routine customer documentation, and limit partner data-room access to what the specific role requires.

Layer three: manufacturing and partner segmentation. No partner in India should receive the entire IP stack unless that is commercially unavoidable and legally protected. The safest architecture is modular: one entity handles sales, another assembly, another service, with the most sensitive software, catalyst, recipe, firmware or tooling retained by the parent or a tightly controlled affiliate. Where local assembly or localisation is necessary to meet procurement or tax requirements, Dutch firms should prefer split BOMs, encrypted firmware updates, black-box subassemblies, controlled calibration tables and function-specific licences over indiscriminate technology transfer. This is particularly important in semiconductors, medtech, industrial machinery, chemicals and specialised manufacturing.

Layer four: enforcement readiness. Firms lose many India IP disputes not because the law is absent, but because evidence hygiene is poor. Enforcement readiness means keeping registration files current, documenting first use, preserving version control, keeping clean employee and contractor invention-assignment chains, maintaining dated confidentiality records, logging suspicious procurement requests, and creating a standing escalation protocol. The legal team should know in advance when to use warning letters, customs alerts, civil injunctions, criminal complaints, tender protests, distributor termination or arbitration. Since the FTA strengthens the broad enforcement environment but does not litigate your case for you, evidence-led readiness is the decisive operational layer.

Sample IP and confidentiality language

“The Recipient shall use the Confidential Information solely for the Permitted Purpose and shall not, without the Disclosing Party’s prior written consent, analyse, decompile, reverse engineer, disassemble, decrypt, benchmark for external publication, reproduce except as strictly necessary, or permit access to the Confidential Information other than by personnel and approved subcontractors bound by written obligations no less protective than those set out herein. The Recipient shall implement appropriate technical and organisational safeguards to prevent unauthorised access, copying, extraction or disclosure, and shall notify the Disclosing Party without undue delay upon becoming aware of any actual or suspected compromise.”

“All Intellectual Property Rights in and to the Products, Software, Documentation, drawings, process parameters, training materials, modifications and derivative works, save for rights expressly assigned in writing, shall remain vested in the Supplier or its licensors. No implied licence is granted. The Buyer shall not register, challenge, assist in challenging or cause any third party to challenge the Supplier’s Intellectual Property Rights, nor use the Supplier’s marks, designs or confidential manufacturing information other than as expressly permitted.”

Tax, customs, local presence, M&A and compliance

The agreement expressly preserves rights and obligations under existing tax conventions; India’s official FAQ states that tax treaties prevail in case of inconsistency. For Dutch firms, that means the FTA should not be expected to solve withholding-tax, permanent-establishment or treaty-benefit questions. Those remain matters for the India–Netherlands tax treaty, Indian domestic tax law, Dutch tax rules and transaction structure. The correct internal governance move is to separate the trade-policy case from the tax case and require both to sign off before the business case goes to the board.

On customs and indirect tax, firms should remember a simple legal proposition: even when FTAs eliminate customs duty, they do not automatically eliminate domestic indirect taxes, compliance filings, valuation issues or non-tariff charges. The Indian local-content order itself defines imported content by including customs duties and excluding net domestic indirect taxes from total value, which illustrates how sharply customs and internal tax treatment can affect procurement and margin calculations. This is precisely why Dutch firms should model both gross border cash impact and recoverable-tax impact rather than relying on a single “tariff saving” number.

Local presence decisions should be made functionally. If the primary value lies in goods export with limited after-sales service and clean origin, direct export may remain optimal. If procurement access, local assembly, servicing, regulatory licensing or data handling become central, then a local subsidiary, branch, JV or acquisition may be preferable. The correct choice should be driven by four variables: the need for local content, the need for licences or branch presence, control over IP and compliance, and the ability to finance and enforce. Services sectors such as banking, telecom and environmental services require especially close alignment between treaty schedule, domestic licensing and investor-control model.
