Abbreviations: M&E = machinery and electrical equipment; Air = aircraft and spacecraft; OptMed = optical, medical and surgical equipment; Chem = chemicals; Plast = plastics; Veh = motor vehicles; Iron = iron and steel; Pharma = pharmaceuticals. Current indicative Indian tariff caps for these sectors, as published by the Commission, are 44%, 11%, 27.5%, 22%, 16.5%, 110%, 22% and 11% respectively. The labels are deliberately abbreviated to avoid the readability problem found in some earlier drafts.

The public tariff examples are commercially instructive. For EU industrial exporters, the Commission highlights chemicals up to 22%, cosmetics up to 22%, plastics with many lines liberalised after seven years, car parts mostly liberalised after five to ten years, machinery liberalised partly at entry into force and the rest over up to ten years, and boats mainly liberalised immediately. The Commission’s Q&A adds concrete examples: machinery and electrical equipment with 2024 exports of €16.3 billion faced current tariffs up to 44%; motor vehicles of €1.6 billion faced 110%; chemicals of €3.2 billion faced up to 22%; pharmaceuticals of €1.1 billion faced 11%. For Dutch companies in capital goods, process technology and specialised equipment, these are not marginal changes but potentially order-winning differences.

Agri-food is more nuanced. The Commission summary indicates duty elimination on olive oil, non-alcoholic beer and fruit juices, processed foods and sheep meat, and tariff reductions on alcoholic beverages and some fruits, while preserving EU sensitivities in sugar, ethanol, rice, soft wheat, beef, poultry, milk powders, bananas and honey. India’s market remains sensitive, and Dutch agri-food exporters should read any market opening together with SPS administration, certification and establishment-listing procedures. The presence of a new Sustainable Food Systems chapter is commercially relevant because it creates a structured cooperation platform without imposing a wholesale harmonisation obligation; India’s own FAQ emphasises that it is a cooperation framework, not a backdoor conditionality instrument.

Rules of origin will be central to actual preference take-up. The Commission summary states that the origin rules are aligned with recent EU FTA practice, require significant processing, use self-certification through a statement on origin, and permit customs verification through a portal and administrative cooperation between customs authorities before preference is refused. For Dutch firms this means two things. First, origin administration should be treatable at scale through supplier declarations, ERP flags and document retention rather than being an impossible manual burden. Secondly, firms with Asian supply chains that currently rely heavily on non-EU components may fail to qualify unless procurement, engineering and transfer-pricing teams redesign sourcing or assembly steps. The real origin discipline is commercial and organisational, not purely legal.

Customs and trade facilitation are often underestimated in board discussions. The Commission summary provides for transparency, advance rulings, simplified procedures and expedited release of goods, and a future-proof legal basis for deeper cooperation on supply-chain security and customs data exchange. Combined with the India–Netherlands bilateral agreement on mutual administrative customs assistance signed in May 2026, Dutch firms should expect a more supportive institutional environment for legitimate trade, but also more sophisticated customs scrutiny where risk flags arise. In practice, better facilitation and stronger control travel together.

SPS and TBT should be treated as core market-access chapters, not technical appendices. The SPS chapter is subject to bilateral dispute settlement and is described as introducing clear timelines and mechanisms for import conditions, approvals, audits, regionalisation, establishment listing, certification and information exchange. The TBT chapter similarly imports WTO TBT commitments into bilateral dispute settlement, requires 60 days of public consultation on new technical regulations, six months between publication and entry into force, and creates a dedicated working group on conformity assessment, explicitly including India’s Quality Control Orders. For Dutch agri-food, machinery, electronics, chemicals, medtech and industrial exporters, those provisions can materially reduce regulatory ambush risk, but only if the firm actively monitors consultations and uses the institutional windows created by the agreement.

The BIS compulsory-certification framework remains highly relevant. BIS states that certification is generally voluntary, but that for a number of products compliance with Indian Standards is made compulsory by the Government in the public interest, including health, safety, environmental protection, prevention of unfair trade practices and national security. The TBT working group’s explicit mandate to address conformity-assessment issues, including Quality Control Orders, is therefore one of the most commercially meaningful non-tariff elements in the FTA for Dutch industrial exporters. The FTA may not abolish India’s standards architecture, but it gives exporters more procedural tools against abrupt or opaque implementation.

Trade remedies remain alive. The Commission makes clear that the agreement confirms continued availability of anti-dumping, anti-subsidy and global safeguard instruments, and adds a bilateral safeguard mechanism where a significant increase of preferential imports causes or threatens serious injury. That coexistence of liberalisation and trade defence is standard modern FTA practice. Dutch firms should therefore not assume that tariff preferences immunise them from investigations; the correct approach is to integrate origin planning with trade-remedy monitoring, especially in sectors such as chemicals, metals, fibres, industrial intermediates and cables. The EU’s own 2025 action against unfairly subsidised Indian optical fibre cables is a practical reminder that liberalisation and enforcement will coexist.

Services liberalisation is one of the most strategically important parts of the package for the Netherlands. The Commission states that services trade was €59.8 billion in 2024, with EU exports of €26 billion, and that the services chapter improves predictability, adopts rules beyond India’s existing GATS position, clarifies local-presence and board/senior-management commitments, and includes some of the most ambitious mobility provisions each side has previously granted. India’s official FAQ adds granular detail: commercially meaningful market-access and national-treatment commitments across 102 sectors/subsectors for the EU; specific EU-interest sectors including maritime transport, financial services, telecom and environmental services; market access for bank branches for EU banks in the amount of fifteen branches over four years; and commitments of 100% FDI in insurance and 74% in banking services. Dutch banks, insurers, maritime operators, logistics firms, engineering consultancies, environmental-services providers and digital service vendors should therefore read the FTA primarily as a platform for deployable operating models, not only a tariff instrument.

Mode structure matters. India’s FAQ confirms commitments on temporary entry and stay for business visitors, short-term business visitors, intra-corporate transferees, contractual service suppliers, independent professionals and family members of ICTs; it also confirms EU commitments in 37 sectors/subsectors for CSS and 17 for independent professionals, with 12-month cumulative stays for CSS and independent professionals, and up to three years extendable for managers and specialists under ICT regimes subject to domestic law. For Dutch firms operating India delivery centres, engineering deployments, project teams and GCC-linked models, this creates a more dependable mobility framework, although immigration, recognition of qualifications and domestic labour rules will still require close management.

Professional services remain mixed. India expressly states that it preserved policy space in areas such as legal services, while both sides agreed to engage on recognition of professional qualifications where that is institutionally feasible. Dutch legal and accounting firms should therefore not read the FTA as a simple opening of the Indian legal market. The likely near-term benefits are greater transparency, better conditions for foreign-law advisory structures, improved corporate demand triggered by trade and investment flows, and—possibly over time—profession-specific recognition arrangements. But full-scale domestic-practice rights should not be assumed from the public materials currently available.

Digital trade is a major improvement, but not a clean override of domestic data regulation. The Commission states that the digital chapter supports a predictable and secure digital trade environment, integrates most WTO e-commerce JSI rules, protects software source code against mandatory disclosure, and contains rules on online consumer protection and spam, while preserving the right to regulate for privacy and security. At the same time, India’s Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 authorises the Central Government to restrict cross-border transfers of personal data to notified countries or territories, and preserves the operation of stricter sector-specific laws. Significant Data Fiduciaries may also face additional obligations, including a Data Protection Officer who must be based in India. In consequence, Dutch ICT, SaaS, medtech, fintech and connected-device firms should treat the FTA as a material improvement in digital-trade doctrine, but not as a blanket pre-emption of India’s domestic privacy and sectoral data rules.
