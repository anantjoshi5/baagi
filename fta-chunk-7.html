For renewable-energy and infrastructure projects, blended finance should be considered where Dutch technology is strong but Indian state-level payment risk, localisation demands or tender design weaken pure private-bank appetite. In that context, the FTA’s existence can still matter because customs predictability, services access and regulatory dialogue improve the underlying risk narrative even if subsidy or procurement access remains imperfect. Boards should therefore direct treasury and business teams to incorporate FTA benefits in the base case of project modelling, but keep availability of preference, origin qualification, local-content compliance and regulatory permits as conditions precedent in financing documentation.

Procurement bidding tips

India’s public procurement remains one of the most commercially promising but legally demanding areas for foreign suppliers. The FTA as published does not contain a dedicated procurement chapter, while India’s domestic procurement framework continues to operate through the revised Public Procurement (Preference to Make in India) Order and the GeM ecosystem. Under that order, local content is defined as the value added in India as a percentage of total value, excluding net domestic indirect taxes and subtracting imported content including customs duties; Class-I local suppliers generally require at least 50% local content, Class-II at least 20%, unless a higher threshold is prescribed. In many tenders, only Class-I suppliers may bid where local capacity is deemed sufficient; even where non-local bidders can participate, purchase preference mechanisms favour Class-I local suppliers within defined price margins.

A practical Dutch procurement playbook should therefore proceed in six steps. First, determine whether the target procurement is purely commercial, quasi-public or fully public and whether it runs through GeM or another tender route. Secondly, classify the offered item or service against the applicable local-content regime and check whether the relevant ministry has prescribed a sector-specific threshold above the default 50% / 20% classification. Thirdly, calculate local content using India’s formula rather than any internal “value added” shorthand used in Europe. Fourthly, decide before bid submission whether to bid as non-local, Class-II or Class-I and ensure the supply chain actually matches that self-description. Fifthly, for contracts above INR 10 crore, prepare audited local-content certification, because the revised order requires accountant or auditor certification and allows penalties of up to 10% of contract value if the promised local-content status is not maintained. Sixthly, align the tender structure with performance-bond, warranty, tax and entity requirements from the first draft of the bid, rather than papering them later.

For bid governance, Dutch firms should use a tender-control matrix with five mandatory internal gates: legal sign-off on eligibility; origin and customs review; local-content calculation sign-off; sanctions and export-control screening; and finance sign-off on bonds, tax gross-up exposure and payment security. This is not bureaucratic excess. Indian public procurement commonly compresses time between bid clarifications and final pricing; errors in supplier status, OEM certification or localisation assumptions are therefore expensive and usually discovered too late. Where bids depend on a local partner or assembler, the partner agreement should be conditional on bid outcome, data access, audit rights and anti-circumvention protections.

The scale of the GeM ecosystem is itself a reason for Dutch firms not to ignore the channel, even though access is mediated by domestic rules. Publicly available figures cited by GeM-linked statistics indicate a very large and still expanding procurement platform. But foreign firms should resist the temptation to treat GeM as an online marketplace equivalent to ordinary B2B e-commerce. It is better understood as a regulated procurement interface embedded in India’s local-content and public-finance architecture. The practical implication is simple: success depends less on catalogue upload and more on structure—local supplier status, documentation, certifications, pricing discipline and consortium design.

Specific contract clause examples

The following clauses are restored and refined as commercially usable model text. They are illustrative drafting language and must be adapted to governing law, sector regulation and transaction structure.

Preferential-tariff activation clause

“If, after the Effective Date, the importation of the Goods into India becomes eligible for preferential tariff treatment under the Free Trade Agreement between the European Union and India, the Parties shall cooperate in good faith to obtain and maintain such preferential treatment. The Supplier shall provide, subject to reasonable notice, the origin statements, supplier declarations and supporting documents reasonably necessary to claim preference. Unless otherwise expressly agreed, any customs duty savings obtained through such preference shall accrue to the Buyer to the extent the Buyer is importer of record; provided that the Contract Price shall be equitably adjusted where the Supplier incurs additional verified compliance costs directly attributable to origin qualification and documentary maintenance.”

Origin and customs cooperation clause

“Each Party shall maintain accurate and complete customs, origin, classification, valuation and shipping records relating to the Goods for the longer of: (i) the period required under applicable law; and (ii) five years from importation. Each Party shall promptly notify the other of any customs query, post-clearance audit, verification request, suspected origin non-compliance or circumstance that may affect preferential treatment. The Parties shall provide each other with reasonable assistance, including technical descriptions, bills of materials, supplier declarations, manufacturing records and shipment data, to support customs clearance, origin verification and administrative review. Neither Party shall make any customs declaration on behalf of the other without prior written approval.”

Regulatory-change and certification clause

“If, after the Effective Date, any law, regulation, standard, conformity-assessment requirement, Quality Control Order, import condition, licence requirement, certification standard or other measure of general application in India materially affects the importation, installation, use, maintenance, sale or continued compliance of the Goods or Services, the affected Party shall notify the other without undue delay. The Parties shall meet promptly to agree such technical, documentary, pricing, delivery or implementation adjustments as are reasonably necessary to preserve the commercial balance of this Agreement. Pending agreement, each Party shall continue performance to the extent legally possible, and the affected delivery dates shall be extended to the extent reasonably attributable to such regulatory change.”

Data-processing and localisation clause

“Each Party shall comply with applicable data-protection, cybersecurity and data-localisation laws in relation to personal data and regulated operational data processed under this Agreement. Where applicable law in India requires specified data to be processed, stored, mirrored or made accessible within India, the Parties shall implement a lawful technical and contractual arrangement to satisfy such requirement without unnecessary disruption to performance. The Customer shall identify in writing any regulated data category known to it before such data is transferred to the Supplier. The Supplier shall not be required to accept localisation obligations that materially increase cost or legal risk unless the Parties agree an equitable adjustment and a compliant processing architecture.”

Public-procurement and local-content clause

“Where performance of this Agreement is intended to support a tender or supply to a public-sector or public-procurement entity in India, each Party shall provide the information reasonably required to determine supplier classification, local-content percentage, country of origin and eligibility under applicable procurement rules. No representation is made that the Goods, Services or Works qualify as ‘Class-I local supplier’ or ‘Class-II local supplier’ unless expressly stated in Schedule [X]. If local-content status is represented, the representing Party shall maintain auditable records supporting that representation and shall indemnify the other Party against penalties or losses arising from its wilful misstatement or negligent miscalculation.”

Forum and change-in-law clause

“This Agreement and any non-contractual obligations arising out of or in connection with it shall be governed by the laws of [chosen jurisdiction], excluding its conflict-of-laws rules. Any dispute, controversy or claim arising out of or relating to this Agreement, including any question regarding its existence, validity or termination, shall be finally resolved by arbitration under the [chosen institutional rules] by [one/three] arbitrator(s), seated in [seat]. Nothing in this Agreement shall prevent a Party from seeking urgent interim or conservatory relief before any competent court. The Parties acknowledge that any treaty-based rights under the Free Trade Agreement between the European Union and India are rights as between states and do not displace the dispute-resolution mechanism agreed in this Agreement.”

These clauses are particularly important because the FTA itself does not create directly enforceable private rights and because operational disputes in India-facing trade usually arise through customs, origin, certifications, data architecture, procurement status or payment security rather than through abstract treaty interpretation.
